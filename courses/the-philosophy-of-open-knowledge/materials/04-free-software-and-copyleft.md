The digital chapter of this history begins with a printer.

In 1980, **Richard Stallman**, a programmer at MIT's AI Lab, was refused the
source code needed to fix a jamming laser printer — the software had become
proprietary, a trade secret. The frustration crystallized into a conviction:
software, like mathematics, is knowledge, and knowledge you cannot inspect,
learn from, fix or share makes you dependent and unfree.

In 1983 Stallman announced the **GNU Project**, an effort to build a complete
operating system that anyone could use, study, modify and redistribute. In
1985 he founded the **Free Software Foundation** and articulated the **four
freedoms** that define free software:

- **Freedom 0** — run the program for any purpose.
- **Freedom 1** — study how it works and change it (source code required).
- **Freedom 2** — redistribute copies to help others.
- **Freedom 3** — distribute your modified versions to the community.

"Free as in freedom, not as in free beer": the point was never price but
autonomy. His masterstroke was legal, not technical: the **GNU General
Public License** (1989) used copyright *against* enclosure. Anyone may use
and modify GPL code, but derivatives must remain free — a mechanism called
**copyleft**. The gift protects itself.

In 1991 a Finnish student, **Linus Torvalds**, released a kernel called
Linux under the GPL, and the combination — GNU tools plus the Linux kernel,
built by thousands of volunteers coordinating over the Internet — grew into
the software that today runs most of the world's servers, phones and
supercomputers. In 1998 the term **open source** was coined to present the
model to business; Eric Raymond's *The Cathedral and the Bazaar* (1997)
explained why openness was not just ethical but *practically superior*:
"given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow".

One more thread matters here. In 1990 the political scientist **Elinor
Ostrom** published *Governing the Commons*, demonstrating with decades of
field evidence that communities can manage shared resources sustainably —
that the "tragedy of the commons" is not a law of nature but a failure of
governance. She received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2009, as digital
commons like Linux and Wikipedia were proving her point at planetary scale.

Free software supplied the missing piece of the open-knowledge tradition:
**a working legal and social technology for building commons on purpose.**
Everything in the next lesson stands on it.
