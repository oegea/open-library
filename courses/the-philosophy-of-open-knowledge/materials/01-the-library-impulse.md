Long before anyone spoke of "open source" or "free culture", human beings
were already doing something remarkable: gathering knowledge in one place so
that it would outlive them.

## Nineveh: a library crosses twenty-five centuries

In the seventh century BC, the Assyrian king **Ashurbanipal** — one of the
few Mesopotamian rulers who could read and write himself, and proud of it —
assembled at Nineveh a library of tens of thousands of clay tablets. He sent
scribes across his empire with orders to find and copy everything worth
keeping: medicine, astronomy, mathematics, dictionaries between languages,
rituals, contracts, and literature — including the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the
oldest great work of literature we possess.

Then Nineveh fell, the library burned, and here is the beautiful irony: the
fire that destroyed the palace *baked the clay tablets harder*, preserving
them under the rubble. When archaeologists unearthed them in the nineteenth
century, humanity recovered texts that had been unreadable for two and a
half millennia — among them the flood narrative of Gilgamesh, which stunned
Victorian Europe. A library had done exactly what libraries are for: it had
carried knowledge across a gap of time that no single life, no single
civilization, could bridge.

## Alexandria: the attempt to collect everything

A few centuries later, the **Library of Alexandria** attempted something
even more audacious: to collect *everything*. The Ptolemies, the Greek
dynasty ruling Egypt, treated knowledge acquisition as state policy. They
funded the library and its associated research institution — the *Mouseion*,
from which we get the word "museum" — where salaried scholars lived, ate and
argued together. According to the physician Galen, writing centuries later,
ships docking at Alexandria's port were searched for scrolls, which were
seized and copied for the collection; the story goes that the library
sometimes kept the originals and returned the copies.

What matters is not the exact scroll count (ancient sources exaggerate
wildly, and modern estimates vary from tens of thousands to several hundred
thousand) but what happened *around* the shelves. **Euclid** organized
geometry into the *Elements*, a textbook still teachable today.
**Eratosthenes**, the chief librarian, calculated the circumference of the
Earth using shadows, wells and geometry — and got astonishingly close.
**Aristarchus** proposed that the Earth orbits the Sun, eighteen centuries
before Copernicus. The library was not a warehouse; it was an engine.

Its end disappoints anyone looking for a single villain. There was no one
great fire, whatever the legend says: Alexandria's collection declined over
centuries of shrinking budgets, political purges, civil wars and neglect —
which is, if anything, a more useful warning. Knowledge is not usually
destroyed in a night. It is defunded, dispersed and forgotten, quietly. But
Alexandria's idea proved indestructible: **a civilization is measured by
what it makes available to those who want to learn.**

## Baghdad: knowledge moves when someone pays to move it

The pattern repeats wherever knowledge flourishes. In eighth- and
ninth-century Baghdad, the Abbasid caliphs sponsored a massive **translation
movement** — remembered through the storied *House of Wisdom*, though modern
historians debate that institution's exact shape. For roughly two centuries,
patrons paid handsomely for Greek, Persian and Indian works of science and
philosophy to be rendered into Arabic. Translation was prestigious,
well-funded work, and the results transformed the world's knowledge:
Aristotle, Euclid, Ptolemy and Galen survived in Arabic through centuries
when Western Europe had largely lost them, later returning to Europe through
Spain and Sicily to help ignite the Renaissance.

The movement also added much of its own. *Algebra* takes its name from
al-Khwārizmī's treatise on *al-jabr*; the word *algorithm* descends from the
Latin rendering of his name. This is worth pausing on: two of the most
fundamental words in modern computing are monuments to a ninth-century
publicly funded knowledge-sharing program.

## What these projects have in common

Across three civilizations and fifteen centuries, notice what repeats:

- **They were deliberate and expensive.** Someone with resources decided
  that collecting, copying and translating knowledge was worth paying for —
  not as charity, but as the foundation of a civilization's strength.
- **They were for others**, including others not yet born. Ashurbanipal's
  scribes could not have imagined the British Museum; they copied anyway.
- **Their value came precisely from not being hoarded.** A sealed library
  is a tomb. These were workshops, staffed and used.
- **They were fragile in the same way.** Each declined not by catastrophe
  but by the slow withdrawal of the will to sustain them.

This is the oldest lineage Open Knowledge belongs to: the simple, ancient
conviction that knowledge kept is knowledge lost, and knowledge given away
is knowledge multiplied. The tools change — clay tablets, papyrus,
parchment, print, git repositories — but the impulse, and the fragility,
do not.
