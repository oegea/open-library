Why do people give knowledge away at all? Not as an occasional act of
charity, but reliably, at scale, for centuries? The question has serious
anthropological answers, and they are the deepest foundation this course
can offer.

## Mauss: the gift creates a bond

In his 1925 essay *The Gift*, **Marcel Mauss** synthesized the ethnography
of gift economies — Malinowski's account of the *kula* exchange in the
Trobriand Islands, where shell ornaments circulate perpetually between
islands in opposite directions; the potlatch of the Pacific Northwest,
where chiefs competed in magnificence of giving — and found that gifts are
never merely economic. A gift creates a **relationship**: an implicit triad
of obligations — to give, to receive, to reciprocate. Refusing any of the
three is a social rupture. In gift cultures, status flows not to those who
hoard but to those who give well.

Sound familiar? It is precisely the economy of free software, of Wikipedia
editing, of academia at its best: **reputation through contribution**. The
hacker who shares a brilliant library, the editor who writes a definitive
article, the scientist who publishes the dataset — they are not being
irrational. They are participating in one of the oldest social
technologies humanity has, one that market economics did not replace but
merely papered over.

Two later books sharpen the point. Lewis Hyde's *The Gift* (1983) argued
that art *must* live partly in a gift economy — that whatever a poem costs
in the shop, the thing that matters about it reaches you as a gift, and
dies if treated purely as a commodity. And the sociologist Richard
Titmuss, in *The Gift Relationship* (1970), compared blood donation
systems and found something startling: Britain's purely voluntary system
produced blood that was safer and supplies that were more reliable than
systems that paid donors — paying, it turned out, drove away the careful
and attracted the desperate, and crowded out the very motive that made the
gift trustworthy. **Some things work better as gifts.** Not despite being
free, but because of it. Knowledge, this course has been arguing for six
lessons, is one of them.

## The refusal to close the loop

A gift differs from a market transaction in one crucial way: **it refuses
to close the loop**. A sale ends the relationship — money for goods,
obligations extinguished, done. A gift leaves something open, pointing
forward: *pass it on*. The reader in a Carnegie library was never asked to
repay Carnegie; the expectation was that an educated person enriches
everyone around them. Economists dryly call this "positive externalities".
The tradition we have traced simply calls it the point.

Notice that every institution in this course is built on that refusal.
Copyleft is reciprocity written into law: you received freedom, you must
pass freedom on. Wikipedia's share-alike license does the same. Even the
public library's silent contract — take a book, return it, bring effort —
is Mauss's triad, administered with a date stamp.

## The Open Knowledge manifesto

**Open Knowledge is built inside this tradition, deliberately.** Its
manifesto, in brief:

- The Internet distributed knowledge universally; AI now lets us
  structure, synthesize and translate it at a scale that was recently
  unthinkable. That capability can feed content mills — or it can power
  gifts. The tool is the same; the tradition you join is a choice.
- Anyone should be able to deploy a library, curate what they know, and
  offer it. **As a gift, not a business.**
- **The learner is not a product.** No names, no emails, no profiles, no
  tracking. If someone just wants to read, they read. This is the public
  library's ethic of open doors *and* reader privacy, ported to the web.
- AI assistance is welcome and **always disclosed**, and sources are
  always credited: the tool structures knowledge; it must never erase
  where knowledge came from. (You are reading an AI-assisted course that
  tells you so on its cover — that is the policy working.)
- Quality is part of the ethics. A free gift that feels shabby teaches
  people that gifts are worth less. Open Knowledge insists that an open,
  self-hostable, free tool can feel *better* than a commercial product —
  because the gift tradition deserves good tailoring.

## The license, and the compass

A word about the license, because it matters. Open Knowledge is released
under the **MIT license**, one of the most permissive that exist: legally
you may do almost anything with it, including commercial use. That is
intentional — permissionless adoption is how tools spread, and your
library should never depend on anyone's approval, including ours.

But a license is a floor, not a compass. **The intent of the project is
not to enable paywalled course platforms or knowledge-as-product
businesses.** If you build a library with this tool, the tradition you are
joining — Nineveh, Alexandria, the Encyclopédie, the Public Libraries Act,
Carnegie, GNU, Wikipedia, arXiv — points one way: give it away.

Someone deploys a library. Publishes knowledge. Someone else walks in. And
learns. That sentence is the entire business model, and it is twenty-seven
centuries old.
