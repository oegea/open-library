Why do people give knowledge away at all? The question has a serious
anthropological answer.

In his 1925 essay *The Gift*, **Marcel Mauss** synthesized the ethnography
of gift economies — Malinowski's account of the Pacific *kula* exchange, the
potlatch of the Pacific Northwest — and found that gifts are never merely
economic. A gift creates a **relationship**
— an implicit triad of obligations: to give, to receive, to reciprocate. In
gift cultures, status flows not to those who hoard but to those who give
well. Sound familiar? It is precisely the economy of free software, of
Wikipedia editing, of academia at its best: **reputation through
contribution**. The hacker who shares a brilliant library, the editor who
writes a definitive article — they are not being irrational; they are
participating in one of the oldest social technologies humanity has.

A gift differs from a market transaction in one crucial way: **it refuses to
close the loop**. A sale ends the relationship — money for goods, done. A
gift leaves something open, pointing forward: *pass it on*. The Carnegie
library's reader was never asked to repay Carnegie; the expectation was that
an educated person enriches everyone around them. Economists dryly call this
"positive externalities". The tradition we have traced simply calls it the
point.

**Open Knowledge is built inside this tradition, deliberately.** Its
manifesto, in brief:

- The Internet distributed knowledge universally; AI now lets us structure,
  synthesize and translate it at a scale that was recently unthinkable.
  That capability can feed content mills — or it can power gifts.
- Anyone should be able to deploy a library, curate what they know, and
  offer it. **As a gift, not a business.**
- **The learner is not a product.** No names, no emails, no profiles, no
  tracking. If someone just wants to read, they read.
- AI assistance is welcome and **always disclosed**, and sources are always
  credited: the tool structures knowledge; it must never erase where
  knowledge came from.
- Quality is part of the ethics: a free gift that feels shabby teaches
  people that gifts are worth less. Open Knowledge insists that an open,
  self-hosted, free tool can feel *better* than a commercial product.

A word about the license, because it matters. Open Knowledge is released
under the **MIT license**, one of the most permissive licenses that exist:
legally, you may do almost anything with it, including commercial use. That
is intentional — permissionless adoption is how tools spread. But a license
is a floor, not a compass. **The intent of the project is not to enable
paywalled course platforms or knowledge-as-product businesses.** If you
build a library with this tool, the tradition you are joining — Alexandria,
the Encyclopédie, Carnegie, GNU, Wikipedia — points one way: give it away.

Someone deploys a library. Publishes knowledge. Someone else walks in. And
learns. That sentence is the entire business model, and it is twenty-seven
centuries old.
