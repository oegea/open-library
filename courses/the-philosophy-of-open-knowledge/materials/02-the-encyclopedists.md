In 1751, in Paris, a bookseller's project became one of the most subversive
acts of the eighteenth century: the ***Encyclopédie*** of **Denis Diderot**
and **Jean le Rond d'Alembert**. Across two decades it grew to twenty-eight
volumes and roughly seventy-two thousand articles, written by more than a
hundred contributors — Voltaire and Rousseau among them.

Its radical idea was not any single article. It was the premise: **all
knowledge, organized and explained, placed in the hands of anyone who could
read**. Trades and crafts — printing, weaving, glassmaking — were documented
with the same care as philosophy, which scandalized those who believed
practical knowledge should stay inside guilds. Diderot wrote that the goal
was "to change the common way of thinking". Authorities agreed it might: the
work was censored, suppressed, and at times printed clandestinely. It kept
going anyway.

The Encyclopédie crystallized several convictions that echo directly into
projects like Wikipedia and Open Knowledge:

- **Knowledge is a common inheritance.** The encyclopedists saw themselves
  as compilers of humanity's shared achievement, not owners of it.
- **Organizing knowledge is itself a service.** Raw information scattered in
  a thousand places serves nobody; structured, cross-referenced explanation
  ("see also…" — their invention of systematic cross-references was
  revolutionary) turns information into learning.
- **Explaining is an act of respect.** Writing so that a curious
  non-specialist can understand is harder than writing for peers, and more
  valuable.

Around the same period, the **Republic of Letters** — the informal network of
scholars exchanging correspondence across borders and confessions — was
demonstrating that knowledge grows fastest when it circulates freely among
people who will never meet. And the slow emergence of the **public domain**
— the idea that copyright is a *temporary* monopoly, after which works belong
to everyone — gave the common inheritance a legal form.

The Enlightenment bet, stated plainly: **a society where knowledge circulates
freely becomes wiser, freer and more prosperous than one where it is
hoarded.** Every open-knowledge project since is a renewal of that bet.
