The nineteenth century turned the old aristocratic library inside out. If
knowledge is a common inheritance, then access to it cannot depend on
wealth — and so, for the first time in history, whole societies began
building libraries **free at the point of use, for everyone, funded as a
public good**.

## The argument, then and always

Britain's **Public Libraries Act of 1850** empowered towns to fund
libraries from local rates. The parliamentary debate is worth remembering
because its arguments have never gone away. Opponents warned that free
libraries would become "unhealthy places of agitation" — that educating
workers would make them restless, and that taxing some to educate others
was theft. Supporters, led by William Ewart, answered that an informed
citizenry was not a threat to society but its foundation, and that the
cheapest police force ever invented was a reading room. The act passed.
Every argument against open access to knowledge you will hear today — it
enables the wrong people, it costs the deserving, it disrupts an industry —
was already rehearsed there, and already answered.

In the United States, the **Boston Public Library** — founded in 1848,
opened in 1854 — established the model of the great free municipal library.
The facade of its 1895 McKim building still declares it "built by the
people and dedicated to the advancement of learning"; over the entrance,
more simply: **"Free to All"**. Three words that would make a fine summary
of this entire course.

## Carnegie: the bottle boy who bought 2,500 libraries

Then came the most spectacular knowledge-philanthropy program ever
executed — and it began with a debt of gratitude. **Andrew Carnegie**
arrived in America as a poor Scottish immigrant boy and went to work at
twelve, first in a cotton mill, then as a telegraph messenger. In
Allegheny, Pennsylvania, a retired merchant named **Colonel James
Anderson** opened his private library of a few hundred volumes to working
boys every Saturday. Carnegie never forgot it. Decades later, having become
one of the richest men alive, he funded a monument to Anderson — and then
funded the experience itself, everywhere: **more than 2,500 free libraries
between 1883 and 1929**, over 1,600 of them across the United States, the
rest from Scotland to Fiji.

In his 1889 essay *The Gospel of Wealth*, Carnegie argued that "the man who
dies thus rich dies disgraced", and placed the free library first among the
worthy uses of a fortune — precisely because, as he saw it, a library helps
only those willing to help themselves: it rewards exactly the effort a
learner brings to it, and nothing else. His method had a condition that
made the gift durable: Carnegie paid for the building, but the community
had to provide the site and commit public funds to operate it forever. The
gift started the institution; the commons maintained it. Most Carnegie
buildings still stand; many still lend books.

Honesty requires the other half of the portrait: the same Carnegie crushed
the Homestead steel strike in 1892, and workers at the time asked, fairly,
whether they were being given libraries with wages that left no time to
read. The tension is instructive rather than disqualifying — it is why the
free software movement, a century later, would insist that freedom must be
built into the *system* rather than depend on the benevolence of any
patron.

## What the movement proved

- **Free access works.** Generations of scientists, writers and inventors
  educated themselves in free libraries, and the societies that built them
  got their investment back many times over.
- **The learner owes nothing but effort.** No fee, no membership of a
  privileged class — and, remarkably, no surveillance. Librarians developed
  a professional ethic of **reader privacy** (what you read is nobody's
  business) more than a century before "data protection" existed as a
  phrase.
- **Institutions built as gifts can outlive their givers** — if a community
  agrees to sustain them. Alexandria's lesson, finally learned.

When Open Knowledge insists that reading requires no registration and that
the learner is not a product, it is not inventing a principle. It is
porting the public library — its openness *and* its discretion — onto the
web.
