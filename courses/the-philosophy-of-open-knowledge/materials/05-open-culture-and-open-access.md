Once free software proved that commons could be engineered, the model
spread from code to culture, education and science within a single decade.

**Creative Commons** (founded 2001 by legal scholar **Lawrence Lessig** with
Hal Abelson and Eric Eldred, first licenses released 2002) did for creative
work what the GPL had done for code: it gave authors a standard, legally
solid way to say "you may share this, and here are the conditions" —
attribution, share-alike, non-commercial — instead of the all-or-nothing of
default copyright. Lessig's *Free Culture* (2004) argued that creativity has
always been built on what came before, and that over-extended copyright was
strangling the very inheritance the Encyclopedists had celebrated. Hundreds
of millions of works now carry CC licenses — including, typically, the
courses published on Open Knowledge instances.

**Wikipedia** (launched January 2001 by Jimmy Wales and Larry Sanger) took
the encyclopedist dream and removed the last barrier: not just free to read
but free to *write*. It became the largest reference work in human history,
in hundreds of languages, produced almost entirely by volunteers — the
clearest proof ever assembled that people will build knowledge commons for
the satisfaction of building them.

Education followed. In 2001 **MIT announced OpenCourseWare**, publishing the
materials of its courses for free — not a diluted sample, the actual
courses. UNESCO coined the term **Open Educational Resources (OER)** in 2002,
and in 2019 its member states unanimously adopted a formal **Recommendation
on OER**, committing governments to promote openly licensed learning
materials worldwide.

Science, whose journals had become paywalled even for the universities that
produced the research, pushed back with the **open access** movement: the
**Budapest Open Access Initiative** (2002) declared that the internet made
it possible for peer-reviewed knowledge to reach "all curious minds" —
"an unprecedented public good". Preprint servers like **arXiv** (1991) and
open journals like **PLOS** made it real.

This chapter also has a martyr. **Aaron Swartz** — programmer, co-author of
RSS at fourteen, early builder of Creative Commons — wrote in his 2008
*Guerilla Open Access Manifesto* that "information is power" and that
sharing scientific knowledge locked behind paywalls was "a moral
imperative". Prosecuted with extraordinary harshness for mass-downloading
academic articles, he took his own life in 2013, at twenty-six. His case
remains the starkest reminder that open access to knowledge is not an
abstract debate.

By the 2010s, the pattern our two previous lessons traced — gift,
institution, commons — existed for every kind of knowledge. What remained
was to make *publishing a curated library* as easy as benefiting from one.
