Once free software proved that commons could be engineered, the model
spread from code to culture, education and science within a single decade.
This lesson covers four fronts of that expansion — and its martyr.

## Creative Commons: a "some rights reserved" for everything

By 2000, default copyright had become automatic, near-eternal (author's
life plus seventy years in much of the world) and all-or-nothing: every
photograph, essay or song was born locked, even when its author would
happily share it. The legal scholar **Lawrence Lessig** — fresh from
losing a Supreme Court challenge to copyright term extension — founded
**Creative Commons** in 2001 with Hal Abelson and Eric Eldred to build the
missing middle ground. The first licenses shipped in December 2002.

CC did for creative work what the GPL had done for code: it gave authors a
standard, legally solid vocabulary of permissions — *attribution* (credit
me), *share-alike* (keep derivatives open, copyleft-style),
*non-commercial*, *no-derivatives* — that anyone can read at a glance.
Lessig's book *Free Culture* (2004) supplied the argument: creativity has
always been built on what came before; a copyright system with no breathing
room strangles the very inheritance the encyclopedists celebrated. Hundreds
of millions of works now carry CC licenses — including Wikipedia, and
typically the courses published on Open Knowledge instances, like this one
(CC BY-SA 4.0: share and adapt freely, credit the source, keep it open).

## Wikipedia: the improbable encyclopedia

**Wikipedia's** origin story is a lesson in humility about process. In 2000
Jimmy Wales launched *Nupedia*, a free encyclopedia written by credentialed
experts through a seven-step review pipeline. In its first year it approved
roughly two dozen articles. As an experiment, in January 2001 Wales and
Larry Sanger bolted on a wiki — a website anyone could edit, instantly, no
committee — intending it as a mere feeder for drafts. Within a month the
wiki had more articles than Nupedia produced in a year; within a decade it
was the largest reference work in human history, in hundreds of languages,
written almost entirely by pseudonymous volunteers.

The encyclopedists' dream, realized beyond their imagining — and proof of
something Ostrom would have recognized: given good rules (verifiability,
neutral point of view, cite your sources) and cheap tools for monitoring
and repair (page histories, watchlists, reverts), an open commons can
outproduce any closed pipeline.

## Open education, open science

Education followed. In 2001, **MIT** faced the question every university
faced: how do we sell our courses online? The faculty committee studying
the question came back with a different answer: we shouldn't. Selling
would compromise what a university is for; the materials should be **given
away**. *OpenCourseWare* was announced that April — the actual materials of
MIT's courses, free to the world — and the practice spread. UNESCO coined
the term **Open Educational Resources (OER)** in 2002, and in 2019 its
member states unanimously adopted a formal **Recommendation on OER**,
committing governments worldwide to promote openly licensed learning
materials.

Science had the furthest to fall and the hardest fight. By the 1990s,
academic publishing had inverted its purpose: researchers did the work
(publicly funded), wrote it up (free), reviewed each other (free) — and
then bought the results back from journal publishers at subscription
prices that rose several times faster than inflation, decade after decade.
Even Harvard's library eventually declared the costs untenable. The
**Budapest Open Access Initiative** (2002) named the alternative: the
internet made it possible for peer-reviewed knowledge to reach "all
curious minds" — "an unprecedented public good". Physicists had quietly
been living it since 1991 on **arXiv**, where papers circulate before and
alongside journal publication; **PLOS** and thousands of open-access
journals followed; today open access is mandated by research funders
across much of the world.

## Aaron Swartz

This chapter has a martyr, and his story should be told plainly. **Aaron
Swartz** helped author the RSS 1.0 specification at fourteen, helped build
Creative Commons' technical infrastructure as a teenager, and spent his
short adult life treating access to knowledge as a moral cause. He
liberated millions of pages of US court records from a pay-per-page
government system. In his 2008 *Guerilla Open Access Manifesto* he wrote
that "information is power" and that sharing locked-up scientific
knowledge was "a moral imperative".

In 2010–11 he used MIT's network to mass-download some 4.8 million
articles from the JSTOR academic archive. JSTOR itself declined to press
charges; federal prosecutors charged him anyway, under an elastic
computer-fraud statute, with felonies carrying decades of theoretical
prison time, and pressed him toward a plea. In January 2013, aged
twenty-six, he took his own life. JSTOR now provides substantial free
public access; his case remains the starkest reminder that open access to
knowledge is not an abstract debate, and that the line between "criminal"
and "founder of the next commons" can be drawn by nothing more than
timing and power.

By the 2010s, the pattern our earlier lessons traced — gift, institution,
commons — existed for every kind of knowledge. What remained was to make
*publishing a curated library* as easy as benefiting from one.
